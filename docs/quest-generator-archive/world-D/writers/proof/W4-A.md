### Branch a × E1 · The heart wins

*Blow a: player · framed · for taking a side in public (`framed-shame`) → door Dispute Public offense. Ending shown: The heart wins.*

- **R1 · `{stall}`.** At the letter-writer's stall in `{stall}`, the heart's own town, the heart, a carter who can't write, disputes with his cart master the season's pay he is saving (Dispute *Broken agreement*), and at his monthly letter he tells the player he wants to bring his daughter home from service with a dowry (his want), and that in one letter he told her where the dowry chest is hidden in the house, "in case the road takes me" (his past), while over the stall hangs the oath board, {board_look}: ∅ "the oath board over the stall, its last line painted out".
- **R2 · `{loft}`.** On the hill by `{clerk_hall}` the player looks for the keeper of the relay loft, whose pigeons have gone a week unfed (Investigate *Disappearance*), "a loft whose pigeons went unfed", while in secret the clerk's lists go to the fences by pigeon from this loft, and when the keeper read one tube and asked about it the letter-writer who writes the lists (the Hand) paid him off the hill and fed the birds himself only when a list was due.
- **R3 · `{robbed_town}`.** At `{robbed_town}` a robbed merchant means to burn his letter-book, "my letters brought the thieves" (Acquire *Copy it before it burns*), and as the player copies it he says the thieves came the week his letter gave the dates of his journey: "robbed the week his letter said he'd be away".
- **Reveal · `{hideout}`.** At `{hideout}` the loft keeper, found in hiding, won't come back, and the leader, off the road, persuades him (Passage *Unwilling passenger*), and he shows the one tube he opened and kept, a list of houses and the weeks they would stand empty in a letter-writer's hand, flown to the fences from his loft, and says a letter-writer of the district paid him off the hill, though not for whom, while the leader adds, from the stalls' carriage book, which she has been reading on the road, that only the clerk of `{clerk_hall}` sends from that loft, so the lists are his sales.
  The tube names them itself: the robbed merchant's house is on it for the week "his letter said he'd be away", it is tied with the loft's own ring, "a loft whose pigeons went unfed", and it is written on the back of a stall's spoiled oath sheet, its last line struck through, as on "the oath board, its last line painted out".
- **Chapter 1's move.** Answering being looked at, the clerk pays a letter-writer he has turned off to read a carter's letter aloud, drunk, in a market square, so the town blames one stall and not a list.
- **C1-1 · `{town}`.** In the market square of `{town}` the turned-off letter-writer, shamed as the drunk who reads letters out (Restore *Lost good name*, cause *exaggerated*), says the clerk told the stalls their reports were a tally for the Wayhouse, and that she was paid for the reading: "a letter-writer paid in Market notes".
- **C1-2 · `{fence_town}`.** But the Market notes lead to a fence's shop in `{fence_town}`, the clerk's buyer, whose boy holds a pigeon tube and doesn't know what's in it (Acquire *Unknown worth*): "a list of houses and dates in a stall's hand", last fair's list, every house on it robbed then.
- **C1-3 · `{fence_town}`.** Therefore on the road out of `{fence_town}` a boy the fence sent into a house that wasn't empty, caught and let go, is hunted by the fence, who thinks he talked (Passage *Fugitive*), and the watch sergeant of `{town}`, whose own house was on a list, asks the player to get the boy out of the fence's reach, and the price, later, is a copy of last fair's list.
- **C2-0 · `{town}`.** At pressure two the player walks `{town}` with its old sign-painter, who knows every door, to see which streets the fences' crews have walked (Threat *Go and look*), and the crews mark nothing, but on every street they walked half the houses have a carter on the road for `{fair}` at `{fair_town}`.
- **Blow and door (a) · `{town}`.** In the market of `{town}` the player is framed for taking a side in public, for the clerk has it said that the player told the watch whose letters said what, and the clients he names stand offended (Dispute *Public offense*), and on the door the town's old sign-painter, who paints every sign and board for the district's halls and stalls, says he painted out the oath's last line ten years ago at the clerk's order: "painted out at the clerk's order".
- **Code-bending way.** The door carries one extra way that takes a side in public: tell one family, privately, who sold their letter.
- **Crisis · at `{town}`.** On the night of `{fair}` every house on this fair's list, flown from the loft on fair eve, has been robbed and the heart's dowry chest is gone, and called to account before the town under the stall's oath board the clerk, {clerk_manner}, says he sold no letter and that the oath binds the writer at the stall and not the hall, "the halls have always known what the stalls read", so in his robbed house the heart gives the card for his daughter's dowry (Dispute *Broken agreement*, the leader as arbiter and the player as his advocate): does the stalls' oath bind the halls?
  - *How it reads blow a:* With the player framed as the Writer who told the watch, any warning the player gave the town counts against the halls, and the leader hears the clerk's charge before she hears the oath.
  - *Standing:* Framed as the Writer who told the watch, the player stands before the leader as the heart's advocate, as any party may name one, and not as the halls' messenger, so the charge is the clerk's to prove and not the player's to answer first.
  - *What the player holds:* The player holds last fair's list from the fence's boy and knows the streets the crews walked, and the leader knows from the stalls' carriage book that the lists are the clerk's, but this fair's houses fly from the loft only on fair eve, a warning to whole streets of absent carters puts the watch in the wrong lane, a Writer telling the watch whose letters said what is the stalls selling secrets, and the clerk sold no letter himself, for whether the oath binds the hall is the very question the leader must judge in public, so fair night lands, and the crisis reads a warning given: if the player warned the watch, the crews took the streets it wasn't in, and the town blames the halls' warning along with their stalls.
  - *The tell:* The paint over the board's last line is newer than the rest, for the oath's first wording survives in the founding book.
  - *The ways:* The ways are taking a side in public, the halls naming their own clerk and standing with the clients (the code-break), the watch sergeant swearing the list's houses and dates against fair night (the price: the list), or the oath's first wording, in the copy of the founding book in `{clerk_hall}`'s charter chest: "nor shall the halls be told".
- **Endings.**
  - *The heart wins* (the oath binds the halls; the clerk struck off; the robbed told who sold them): won by law; who pays: the clerk · the guild's quiet (the halls admit the stalls were read). ← shown
  - *The clerk wins* (the halls keep the stalls' reading; the clerk stays, and says he'll sell no more): won by persuasion; who pays: the heart (no redress) · every stall client.
  - *Take a side at last* (code-break: the halls stand with the clients before `{town}`): won by law; who pays: the guild's neutrality (every stall must now swear before its town).
- **A month on (The heart wins).** The dowry half-repaid from the clerk's takings / the stalls' board repainted, whole, and the clients who were told the player sold them learn who did / the clerk sells his list one last time, to anyone.
